Orcinus orca

Ocean's most coordinated hunter

No two orca pods hunt alike. Each one carries its own dialect, its own strategy, and a matriline of knowledge passed down for generations — making the orca less a species and more a set of distinct cultures beneath the surface.

Matriarchs run the family

Pods are built around grandmothers, not males — one of the few social structures in nature led this way for life.

An orca pod is a matriline: a grandmother, her daughters, and their offspring, often spanning four generations swimming together. Sons stay with their mothers for life — even fully grown 6-ton males still travel at her side.

Post-reproductive females can live decades past their last calf, and it's these grandmothers who carry the pod's memory: where the salmon run late, which strait to avoid, how to hunt a particular prey.

Strategy, not instinct

Every ecotype has evolved its own playbook, taught rather than instinctive.

Wave-washing

Antarctic pods swim in formation to create a wave that knocks seals clean off their ice floe — a coordinated maneuver that takes the whole group to pull off.

Intentional stranding

In Argentina, orcas beach themselves on purpose to snatch sea lion pups from the shore, then use the next wave to slide back into the water.

Carousel feeding

Herring-eating pods herd fish into a tight ball near the surface, then stun them with a slap of the tail before feeding one at a time.

Built for the open water

Every marking and fin shape serves a purpose in the hunt or the pod.

  • 01A saddle patch behind the dorsal fin — as unique as a fingerprint, used by researchers to identify individuals for life.
  • 02An eye patch that may confuse prey about which direction the orca is actually facing.
  • 03A dorsal fin that can stand nearly 6 feet tall in adult males — the tallest of any cetacean.
  • 04Countershading — dark above, pale below — that breaks up their silhouette from both the surface and the depths.

Every ocean, one species

Orcas hold the widest range of any marine mammal apart from humans.

Salish Sea

Resident pods here specialize almost entirely in Chinook salmon, tracking runs with precise seasonal timing.

Norwegian fjords

Pods follow herring migrations into narrow, cold fjords each winter, feeding through the polar dark.

Antarctic pack ice

Specialized "Type B" orcas hunt seals directly off the ice, using wave-washing as a group.

Open Pacific

Transient, mammal-eating pods roam vast distances, hunting seals, sea lions, and even other whales.

A species still being understood

Every ecotype researchers study reveals another layer — distinct dialects, distinct diets, distinct customs passed matriline to matriline. The more closely orcas are studied, the more they look less like one species and more like many cultures sharing a single body plan.
